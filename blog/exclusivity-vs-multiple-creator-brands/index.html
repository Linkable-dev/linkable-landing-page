How to choose between exclusivity and multiple creator brands

Exclusivity feels like protection, but locking in a creator before you know what works usually costs more than it saves. Here's how to decide.

A creator you gifted product to last month just posted a video that drove real sales. Now they have messaged asking if you want them exclusively, no other brands in your category for the length of the deal. It sounds like the obvious next step. It usually isn't, not yet.

How to choose between exclusivity and multiple creator brands

Choose multiple creator relationships over exclusivity until you have a pattern of what works, then only lock in exclusivity with the handful of creators who have already proven they move your product. Exclusivity is a reward for performance you can point to, not a bet you place on potential. For a brand still building out its creator programme, paying a premium to stop one creator talking to a competitor usually buys less protection than it costs in cash or discount on their rate.

What exclusivity actually buys you

An exclusivity clause stops a creator from posting for named competitors, usually for a set period. In exchange, creators expect something back: a higher fee, a longer-term retainer, or a commission rate that makes giving up other brand deals worth it.

That trade only makes sense once you know the creator's content actually sells. Early on, you don't know that yet. You have a feeling, maybe one good video, and a request for a bigger commitment than the relationship has earned. If you want a clear read on whether a creator is worth that kind of commitment before you negotiate anything, start with how to evaluate a creator before working with them.

Why exclusivity costs more early on

Exclusivity concentrates your risk in one person. If that creator's content style stops working, their posting slows down, or they simply move on, you've paid a premium to find that out with no fallback content coming from anyone else.

Working with several creators at once does the opposite. It spreads the risk, and it gives you comparison data you can't get any other way: which angle, which hook, which platform actually converts for your product. A supplement brand running a few creators on the same product can see within a couple of cycles which one's audience actually buys, and which one just gets views. Lock in exclusivity before you have that comparison and you've picked a winner by guesswork.

There's also a quieter cost: exclusivity narrows your pool right when you most need it wide. If you're still working out how many creators your brand should work with each month, tying up budget and goodwill on one exclusive deal makes that harder, not easier.

When exclusivity does make sense

Once a creator has driven measurable, repeatable sales across more than one campaign, exclusivity starts to look like a sensible trade rather than a gamble. At that point you're not betting on a feeling, you're protecting something that already works. This is especially true in categories where a handful of creators dominate the conversation, such as skincare or activewear, where a competitor picking up your best performer is a real loss, not a hypothetical one.

Even then, keep exclusivity narrow. Limit it to your direct competitor set rather than an entire category, and keep the term short enough that you can renegotiate once you have more data. An open-ended, category-wide clause locks you into terms that made sense at signing and may not six months later. If a deal starts to underperform against what you agreed, renegotiate it rather than riding it out.

How to structure a deal that keeps your options open

Instead of exclusivity, most brands get better results from a graduated structure: gifted or low-commitment work first, a paid or affiliate deal once content performs, and exclusivity only as a final tier reserved for creators who have already proven themselves across multiple campaigns. That mirrors how long-term creator partnerships should build anyway, earned stage by stage rather than agreed on day one.

This is also where running a programme through Linkable helps, because you can see content delivered, audiences reached and tracked sales per creator in one place. That comparison is what tells you whether a creator has earned exclusivity, instead of guessing from a single good post.

Say you run a coffee brand working with a mix of creators on a gifted basis. One creator's videos consistently drive orders through their tracked link, while three others get views but no sales. That's the creator worth a conversation about exclusivity. The others stay on the open roster, which is exactly where they should be until they prove otherwise.

Frequently asked questions

Should a new brand ever offer exclusivity to a first creator partner?

Generally not. A first partnership hasn't produced enough data to know if the content actually converts, so exclusivity at that stage is a premium paid on a guess rather than a proven result.

How long should an exclusivity clause last?

Keep it short, often a single campaign cycle or a few months, so you can review performance and renegotiate rather than being locked into terms that no longer reflect the relationship.

Does exclusivity have to mean a whole category?

No. Narrow it to direct competitors rather than an entire category. A broad, category-wide clause removes options you may still need and rarely matches the actual risk you're protecting against.

What should a brand offer instead of exclusivity early on?

A graduated structure works better: start with gifted or affiliate terms, move to paid collaboration once content performs, and reserve exclusivity for creators who have already shown repeatable results.
