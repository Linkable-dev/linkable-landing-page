How to handle a creator who misses a deadline

Chasing a late creator wastes a week you don't have. Here's how to build slack and automatic fallbacks into the brief so a missed deadline never stalls the whole campaign.

You've got a launch date, three creators confirmed, and one of them has gone quiet two days before the content is due. You message. No reply. You message again, this time with a question mark and a slightly different tone. Still nothing. Now you're spending your Tuesday afternoon chasing someone instead of planning the next campaign.

How do you handle a creator who misses a deadline?

Message once, politely, with a specific new date. If you don't hear back within a day or two, activate the fallback you already built into the plan rather than waiting any longer. The mistake most brands make is treating the missed deadline as a problem to solve in the moment, when it should have been planned for before the brief was even sent.

A single follow-up is reasonable. A third message, a phone call, or rewriting the brief to make it easier for them is not your job. Creators miss deadlines for ordinary reasons (a busy week, a change of plan, low motivation for a gifted-only deal) and none of those reasons are things you can fix by being more persistent.

Why does chasing content kill momentum?

Every hour spent following up with one creator is an hour not spent briefing the next one, reviewing content that did arrive, or getting a product live on Shopify. Chasing also puts you in a weak position. You end up negotiating down your own deadline, accepting rushed content you wouldn't normally approve, or quietly dropping the whole collaboration because you've run out of patience and time.

Say you run a coffee brand with a bundle launching on a Friday and four creators briefed for Thursday posts. If one creator goes quiet on Wednesday, spending that day messaging them means you're not reviewing the other three creators' drafts or prepping the product page. The campaign doesn't fail because one person was late. It fails because the whole week got reorganised around one person being late.

How do you build slack into the brief before it's needed?

Set the deadline you tell the creator a few days before the date you actually need the content. If your product page needs the video by the Thursday before launch, tell the creator the deadline is the Monday. That gives you room to chase, swap, or fall back without the whole plan moving.

Put the real date, the buffer, and what happens if content is late directly in the brief, not in a separate message you send later. Our creator brief template for ecommerce covers how to write a brief that sets expectations clearly enough that this rarely becomes a surprise. The brief is also the place to confirm payment terms, because a creator who isn't sure when or how they'll be paid is more likely to deprioritise your deadline. If you haven't settled that, our guide on how to pay creators is worth reading first.

What does a fallback plan look like?

Brief more creators than you strictly need for the launch date, and treat the extras as the fallback rather than a backlog. If you need four pieces of content for Friday, brief six creators with staggered deadlines across the week. The two who come in late become next week's content instead of a gap in this week's plan.

On Linkable, this is easier because applications for a new campaign start coming in within a day, so there's usually a pool of interested creators you can invite quickly if someone drops out. Reporting shows which creators have delivered and which haven't, so you can see the gap and invite a replacement from the same campaign rather than starting outreach from scratch. When should you let a creator go?

If a creator misses the buffer deadline and doesn't respond to one follow-up, move on for this campaign. Don't reopen the conversation a week later asking for the content anyway. Late content that arrives after you've already filled the gap with someone else usually isn't worth the approval time, and accepting it teaches that creator (and anyone who hears about it) that your deadlines are negotiable.

That doesn't mean blocking them from future campaigns. A creator who missed one gifted deadline during a busy month might be reliable on a paid collaboration with a clearer fee attached. Keep the relationship open, just don't let one missed date turn into an open-ended wait that costs you the next campaign too.

This week, go through your current campaign brief and check whether the deadline you've given creators has any buffer in it at all. If it doesn't, add a few days, and confirm you've briefed enough creators that one slow reply doesn't become the whole campaign's problem.

Frequently asked questions

Should you pay a creator who missed the deadline but delivered late content?

Only if the content still has a use, such as for a later campaign or evergreen content library, and even then renegotiate the fee or terms rather than paying the original rate for work that missed its purpose.

How many creators should you brief to cover for possible delays?

Brief more than the minimum you need, with staggered deadlines, so a late or unresponsive creator doesn't leave a gap. The exact number depends on your campaign size, but treating every creator as essential to the one deadline is the riskiest setup.

Does gifted content get delayed more often than paid content?

Gifted collaborations tend to see more delays because there's less financial commitment keeping the creator to the date. Our piece on gifted vs paid creator collaborations covers how to choose based on how reliable you need the timeline to be.

Should the brief include a penalty for missing the deadline?

For paid collaborations, yes, a simple clause tying payment to delivery by the agreed date protects you without needing to chase anyone. Our creator contract essentials for small brands covers what to include.
