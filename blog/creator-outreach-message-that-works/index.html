How to write a creator outreach message that gets replies

Most outreach messages get ignored because they read like a form letter. Here is what to include, what to leave out, and templates that sound like a person wrote them.

You have found a creator whose feed fits your brand. You open a message box and stare at it. Whatever you type next either gets a reply within a day or sits unread in a pile of pitches that all sound the same.

A creator outreach message that gets replies is short, names something specific about the creator's actual content, states exactly what you're offering, and asks for one small next step. Anything longer than a few sentences, anything generic, and anything that asks the creator to do the thinking for you will get skipped.

What should a creator outreach message actually say

Four things, in this order. Who you are and what the brand sells, in one line. What you noticed about their content that made you reach out. What you're proposing (gifted product, a paid fee, or an affiliate commission) and roughly what that looks like. And one clear ask, usually

That's it. No brand history, no mission statement, no paragraph about your values. A creator scanning their inbox between filming and editing does not need your backstory. They need to know fast whether this is worth ten more seconds of reading.

Keep the whole message to something that fits on a phone screen without scrolling. If you have to explain your campaign in three paragraphs, the campaign brief comes later, not in the first message. Save the detail for after they say yes, using something like a creator brief template once you're through this step.

Why does personalisation matter more than the offer

Creators can tell within a sentence whether a message was written for them or copied and pasted to a hundred people that day. A generic message signals a generic relationship, and most creators skip those regardless of what's on offer.

Personalisation doesn't mean flattery. It means proof you watched or read something they made. Reference a specific video, a specific product they've talked about, or a format they use often. One accurate detail does more work than three compliments.

Say you run a coffee brand and you find a creator who posts weekly brew guides. A message that says "we love your content" gets ignored. A message that says "your pour-over comparison video was the reason we reached out, our beans would fit that format well" gets read, because it proves you actually looked.

What should you leave out of the first message

Leave out contracts, usage rights, exclusivity clauses and long lists of deliverables. Those belong in a follow-up once the creator has said they're interested, not in the opening message where they'll just cause hesitation or a straight no.

Leave out vague asks like "would you be interested in collaborating" with no detail on what that means. Vague asks force the creator to ask you three clarifying questions before they can even decide, and most won't bother.

Leave out pressure. Don't mention a deadline in the first line, don't say "limited spots," and don't chase within a day of sending. If you're running campaigns through Linkable, creators can already see the campaign details, product and payment structure before they apply, so your direct message just needs to point them there and add the personal reason you reached out.

How do you follow up without sounding pushy

Wait close to a week before following up, and when you do, add something new rather than repeating the original ask. A short reminder that references a new post they've published, or a slight sweetening of the offer, reads as attentive rather than nagging.

If there's no reply after two messages, stop. Creators get pitched constantly and a lack of response is an answer. Move to the next creator on your list rather than sending a third message that starts to feel like pressure, which damages how your brand is seen even by creators who never respond.

Two templates you can adapt today

Here's a gifted product version and a paid or affiliate version, both short enough to send as a direct message.

Gifted: "Hi [name], loved your [specific video or post], especially [specific detail]. We make [product] and think it would suit your content. Happy to send one over, no obligation to post. Would that be useful?"

Paid or affiliate: "Hi [name], your [specific content] caught our eye, particularly [specific detail]. We're running a creator campaign for [product] with [a fixed fee / commission on sales], and think you'd be a strong fit. Can I send over the details?"

Both versions follow the same shape: specific compliment, clear offer, single question. Swap the bracketed parts for the real creator and product, and don't add anything else. If you want a wider system for finding these creators before you message them, our post on applications versus outbound recruiting covers when to write cold messages like these versus letting creators come to you.

Try rewriting your next five outreach messages this week using the four-part shape above, and track which ones get a reply within a couple of days. That's the fastest way to see which version of your pitch actually works for your brand.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a creator outreach message be

Short enough to read on a phone without scrolling, usually four or five sentences. If your message needs a paragraph to explain the offer, save that detail for a follow-up after the creator responds.

Should you message creators on the platform they post on or by email

Direct messages on the platform where they post usually get read faster, since that's where creators are already active. Email works better once you have a working relationship and need to send contracts or briefs.

Is it better to offer gifted product or a paid fee in the first message

Offer whichever your campaign is actually structured as, and say so plainly. Being vague about payment in the first message is one of the fastest ways to lose a reply.

What's a good sign that an outreach message worked

A reply within a few days that asks a specific question about the product or the campaign, rather than a generic thank you. That signals genuine interest rather than politeness.
